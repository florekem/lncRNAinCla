# Discussion

In this study, using RNA-Seq data, we identified a list of putative lncRNA transcripts involved in the MC-LR induced liver injury of/in whitefish, a non-model specie without a reference genome. Further qPCR validation of selected putative lncRNA candidates confirmed participation of long non-coding transcripts in the MC-LR induced liver injury in whitefish. We show that altered lncRNAs expression profiles could serve as a potential biomarkers of liver injury in whitefish.

The lack of standardized methodologies for discovery of lncRNAs poses a challenge for the analysis and interpretation of RNA sequencing data. The outcome of pipelines designed to discover lncRNAs in RNA-Seq data highly depends on factors which precede *in silico* analysis, such as RNA isolation or preparation method of sequencing libraries [@Chao2019]. Therefore, methods designed for enriching polyadenylated protein-coding mRNAs may not be optimal for recovering very low abundant lncRNA. On the other hand, designing high scale RNA-Seq experiment from a large set of samples is demanding and usually some trade offs must be made [@Sims2014]. Because the main scope of our RNA-Seq experiment was to dissect protein-coding genes profiles, Illuminas TruSeq Stranded mRNA protocol was chosen (Brzuzan et al., in preparation). However, this protocol could also be used for lncRNAs discovery [@Chao2019]. In fact, the majority of biologically functional lncRNAs reported to date are polyadenylated [@Zhang2014c], thus aproaches based on enriching polyadenylated transcripts to discover functional lncRNAs are common in pipelines based on annotated genomes, as well as de novo assembled transcriptomes. For example 54,503 putative lncRNAs was discovered in rainbow trout [@Al-Tobasei2016], and 122,969 putative lncRNAs was reported in *Rhinella arenarum* [@Ceschin2020]. Our pipeline based on the de novo assembly of liver transcriptome allowed us to obtain 209,270 non-coding transcripts longer than 200 nt, from which 84,974 we labeled as putative novel long non-coding transcripts (see further discussion). 

Differences attributed to source of reference genome, studied species or selection of tissues causes difficulties in comparing results, which on top may suffer from poor reproducibility [@Wolfien2016]. Apart form that, to obtain reliable and comparable results in discovery of lncRNAs, sequencing depth should be considered. It is estimated that in human samples >200 million paired-end reads are required to detect the full range of transcripts, including all possible isoforms [@Tarazona2011]. However for differential expression analyses this number can be much lower. For example, if the expectation is that the expression of abundant transcripts changes across conditions, 36 million reads per sample may be sufficient [@Sims2014]. Because it was expected that, (1) MC-LR will drastically change expression profiles of transcripts [] and (2) polyadenylated lncRNAs are expressed at higher abundances than non-polyadenylated lncRNAs [@Kashi2016], we sequenced our liver samples with 50 million reads per sample.

In organisms without a reference genome, quality of the *de novo* assembled transcripts is a prerequisite for obtaining meaningful results of downstream analysis, such as discovery of novel transcripts [@Eldem2017]. Here, we assembled whitefish liver transcriptome from 52 liver samples that originated from different experimental groups, including those which were not part of this study, additionally extending scope of available transcripts used for the assembly (Brzuzan et al., in preparation). BUSCO analysis, which estimates assembly quality based on evolutionary-informed expectations of gene content from orthologues selected from OrthoDB, showed 74.9% completeness to Actinopterygian core genes (OrthoDB v10). In contrast, the current best assembly of whitefish full-length transcriptome based on a whole fish homogenate showed slightly higher 76,6% completeness (OrthoDB v10) [@Carruthers2018]. Importantly, BUSCO recovery tends to be higher in a full organism assemblies compared to those assembled from a select number of tissues. For example whitefish tissue-based full-length transcriptome deposited in PhyloFish database showed only 26% completeness [@Carruthers2018]. Because our liver transcriptome showed completeness comparable to current best whitefish whole transcriptome assemblies, we believe that it not only provided solid foundation for our analysis, but it also could extend completeness of current and future assemblies of whitefish transcriptome.

The importance of designing an accurate long non coding RNA discovery step-by-stem pipeline is essential for producing a high-quality results. This is especially important for non-model species without a reference genome, as redundancy tends to be higher in those analysis. As the state-of-the-art, consensus integrated pipeline does not yet exist, we decided to base our pipeline on the previously described pipeline for identification of lncRNAs in non-model species [@Harris2017]. The pipeline aims to remove known protein coding transcripts (PCTs) and other non coding RNAs in a sequence of filtration steps. As a result the pipeline predicts novel lncRNAs, then using software based on support vector machines (SVMs) attempts to validate them by calculating their coding probability. Unfortunately, as both the pipeline and the validation uses BLAST results with varying levels of confidence, this validation is in fact only pseudo-independent, thus presented transcripts are, at best, predictions and only experimental evidence will validate their true function [@Harris2017]. However, the lack of assessing the sensitivity or the specificity of the pipeline is common among current studies aiming to classify novel lncRNA transcripts, particularly in a non-model species without well-annotated genome. Here we present that the putative lncRNAs look fundamentally different in corrected free energy, length and GC content from protein coding RNA molecules (Figure X). This factors are considered as crucial for RNA stability and are in line with previous reports of lncRNAs having different stability as compared to protein coding transcripts in fish [@Al-Tobasei2016].

Based on similarity of our transcripts to the non coding sequences deposited in the Rfam databse, we identified putative whitefish liver lncRNAs which expression was changed after MC-LR administration (known DElncRNAs). We found that MC-LR altered expression of contigs of potent regulators of genes, such as HOTAIR, HOTTIP, HULC or MALAT1 (Figure X). Generally, lncRNAs are poorly conserved among species [@Wang2004], however MALAT1 is conseved in mammals [@Hutchinson2007a] as well as in other vertebrates, such as zebrafish [@Ulitsky2011a], suggesting that it has important conserved biological roles. Moreover, MALAT1 is one of the most abundantly expressed lncRNAs in normal tissues [@Hutchinson2007; @Sun2019], comparable even with many protein-coding genes, such as GAPDH [@Zhang2012a]. MALAT1 expression has been shown to be either upregulated (lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma) or downregulated (colorectal cancer, breast cancer) [@Sun2019], thereby its role is either cancer-promoting or a tumor-suppressing. Our results show that MALAT1 was present at a high levels in normal whitefish liver and its expression was downregulated at 6 and 9d after MC-LR exposure. At the fuctional level, among different molecular mechanisms, MALAT1 has been shown to bind various miRNAs which in result led to promoting [@Chen2017a] or decreasing cancer progression [@Xia2018]. For example, knocking down MALAT1 significantly upregulated the expression of miR34a [@Li2019a]. Importantly, our previous results demonstrated that miR34a was upregulated in whitefish liver after MC-LR exposure [@Lakomiak2019]. Taken together, our results could indicate that decreased abundance of MALAT1 is linked with upregulated levels of miR34a in whitefish liver after MC-LR exposure, which could potentially underlie the molecular mechanism of MC-LR-induced liver injury.

From methodological perspective, it was demostrated that the majority of MALAT1 transcripts are not polyadenylated [@Wilusz2008]. As we were using library preparation method, that enriches only polyadenylated transcripts, it was not possible for us to detect non-polyadenylated MALAT1 transcripts. However, it has been releaved that in mammals MALAT1 has two isoforms: highly abundant short isoform, which lacks poly(A) tail, and long isoform, that is present in much lower level, but has genomically encoded poly(A) tract. Longer isoform is further processed by RNAse P and RNAse Z to shorter isoform. In our pipeline we might have been detected the polyadenylated isoform of MALAT1. Unfortunately, this limits discussion on biological role of MALAT1 in MC-LR-induced liver injury, but should be adressed in further studies. 

After removal of PCTs and known NCTs we noticed that a large group of remaining non-coding transcripts still maps to the mRNAs deposited in the NCBI non-redundant nucleotide collection (nr/nt). However after closer examination of particular blast hits, we noticed that the vast majority of our remaining non-coding transcripts maps not to the coding parts of matched mRNA sequences, but to their non-coding 3'-UTR regions. The presence of autonomous 3′-UTR transcripts separated from their associated mRNAs was documented in studies on mouse and human cells [@Mercer2011; @Kocabas2015; @Malka2017]. Because 3'-UTR regions that were considered to be a part of the canonical transcripts are in fact biologically significant autonomous units participating in post-transcriptional regulation [@Malka2017], we analyzed how our putative autonomous 3'-UTR transcripts relate to corresponding PCT of the same mRNA. We found that in normal (unchalleged) condition (control group) almost the same number of mRNAs had significantly higher number of 3'-UTR transcripts (48% of DEmRNAs) as those which had higher number of PCT (52% of DEmRNAs). This may indicate that in normal whitefish liver those transcripts remain in stable relationship. Moreover we show that MC-LR exposure increased PCTs while decreasing 3'-UTR transcripts of the same mRNA (Figure 3 b). In pairs which expression was changed after MC-LR exposure, 60% of pairs had more PCTs than 3'-UTR transcripts. In the contrary, the same DE pairs showed the opposite in the control samples (i.e. 60% of the same pairs had more 3'-UTR transcripts), indicating that MC-LR changed the ratio of 3'-UTR/PCT in about 20% of DEmRNAs (depending on day of exposure). This also indicates that expression of our putative 3'-UTR transcripts changed independently of PCTs after MC-LR exposure, which was additionally validated by comparing pattern in expression of 3'-UTR/PCT pairs with pattern of changes in expression of all PCT (paired and unpaired with 3'-UTRs) (FIUGRE X).

In the majority of cases if an putative autonomous 3'-UTR was differently expressed after MC-LR exposure, the associated PCT was also DE in the same direction. Our results show that from all DE 3'-UTR/PCT pairs at 1d, over 82% of paired transcripts were upregulated after the MC-LR exposure (Figure 3a). Subsequent days of exposure (6 and 9d) did not show such substantial shift in the expression in any direction. Moreover, GO terms analysis showed that co-upregulated pairs after 1d of exposure were enriched in transcription regulators, suggesting role of this transcripts in regulating response to severe liver damage. On the contrary, our resutls show similarities in direction of expression and function of DE 3'-UTR/PCT pairs between 6 and 9d of the exposure (Figure X). Differences in ratios of DE pairs between 1 and both 6 and 9d reflects differences observed in all DE PCTs (paired and unpaired) which may suggest potential mechanisms of regulation of PCTs in response to MC-LR. Moreover, our previous study demonstrated that exposure to MC-LR results in severe liver damage, followed by an unexpected resilience to further exposures to the toxin, leading to regeneration of the damaged liver structure [@Wozny2016]. Current study shows that this is refrected in changes in the liver transcriptome, where 1d differs from both 6 and 9d. This reslts lays groundwork for future studies on mechanisms of regulation of PCTs by MC-LR.

Moreover, here we also report that in the response to MC-LR some putative autonomous 3'-UTRs were differently expressed in the opposite direction than coding transcripts of the same mRNA (Figure 3a). We were particularly interested in pairs where 3'-UTR transcripts were upregulated and PCTs were downregulated, as this could be attributed to the recently proposed mechanism of shortening of mRNAs to increase the effectiveness of miRNA binding sites [@Malka2017]. In this scenario levels of 3'-UTRs rises in effect of shortening of mRNAs, and for that reason miRNAs are able to more effectively lower levels of corresponding PCT. Because our previous study showed aberrant expression of miRNAs and their role in the regulation of transcription after exposure to MC-LR [@Brzuzan2016], this hypothesis should be verified in the future work. Furthermore this results suggests the necessity of wider perspective in investigating effects of MC-LR induced liver damage on a molecular level. For example Meng et al. after subsequent studies on MC-LR-induced reproductive toxicity in mice, showed a transcriptomic regulatory network of miRNAs, piRNAs, circRNAs, lncRNAs and mRNAs that were simultaneously involved in cytotoxicity of MC-LR in testicular tissues [@Meng2019a].

Importantly, our discovery of the autonomous 3'-UTRs in whitefish liver may be biased by the highly redundant nature of the *de novo* assembled genomes. Although we showed that the observed putative 3'-UTR contigs were expressed independently from PCTs, some of the autonomous 3'-UTRs paired with PCTs of the same mRNA could in fact be fragmented or incomplete mRNAs which were not assembled properly. On the other hand, even if *de novo* transcriptome assembly approach for detecting various types of non-coding RNAs is not without flaws, we think that it could be also used as an extension to analyses based on annotated genomes. Because the detection of autonomous 3'-UTR transcripts using annotated genome requires additional enrichment of 3'-UTR transcripts at RNA isolation or library preparation steps, it enforces additional sequencing runs. Alternatively, pipelines designed to analyze autonomous 3'-UTRs based on the *de novo* assembled transcriptome may be a backing strategy in preliminary research, eventually leading to more focused sequencing runs. Furthermore already deposited transcriptomic data may be reused for additional *de novo* analysis by teams seeking for direction of their further studies on non-coding RNAs.

The aforementioned group of 84,974 putative novel long non-coding transcripts contained transcripts longer than 200 nucleotides with no homology to any tested database. We showed that MC-LR upregulated expression levels of 1739 putative novel lncRNAs and downregulated 2690 (Figure 6 a,b). Comparing DE novel lncRNA transcripts common between days of exposure to MC-LR with the same groups of DE PCT transcripts (Figure 3 c,d) shows simillar patern in response to MC-LR. Besides already discussed mechanism of MC-LR liver toxicity, this indicates co-DE-expression of lncRNAs and mRNAs in MC-LR-induced liver injury in whitefish. Because as for now it is the most common approach for identifying potential target genes of lncRNAs [@Li2019], we will further be able to identify potential target genes of lncRNAs and to investigate the biological function of lncRNAs in MC-LR-induced liver injury in whitefish.

We further confirmed expression change of selected DElncRNAs with qPCR, which improved the credibility of our methodology. These putative long non-coding RNAs might serve as potential biomarkers for early detection of severe liver injury in whitefish (1d), as well as its progression and resilience to the toxin (6d, 9d). Therefore, in the future studies, it is important to determine whether their dysregulation is also detectable by using noninvasively collected samples such as blood [@Florczyk2016]. 


### Conclusions
Our lncRNA dataset provides the starting point for future lncRNA studies in *C. lavaretus* which would allow to verify them and to assess their regulation and function. Undoubtedly, the future availability of whitefish genome will help curate the lncRNA set using strategies like the identification of promotor regions followed by non-coding regions. Future sequencing of whitefish genome would represent a unique opportunity since RNA-Seq data could be used to curate the genome assembly and vice versa.
